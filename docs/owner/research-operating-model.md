# Universal Company Research & Intelligence Operating Model

## 1. Purpose of This Model

This document defines a **universal Research & Intelligence operating model** designed to work across almost any type of organization.

The objective is not to create a research department that only performs academic research, market research, user research, or scientific R&D.

The objective is to create **one shared research capability that can support every major function of a company**, including:

* Executive leadership
* Corporate strategy
* Product
* Engineering
* Technology
* AI/ML
* Security
* Marketing
* Sales
* Finance
* Legal
* Compliance
* Human resources
* Operations
* Supply chain
* Customer success
* Business development
* Long-term innovation

The model therefore needs to work across radically different questions.

For example:

> Should we enter a new country?

> Why are customers abandoning our product?

> Should we build or buy a particular technology?

> Is our architecture appropriate for our expected scale?

> Is a security control actually reducing risk?

> Why are employees leaving?

> Which market segment should we target?

> Is a new technology mature enough to invest in?

> Should we acquire another company?

These questions require different research methods.

Therefore, the model deliberately **does not force the entire company to use one research methodology**.

Instead, it establishes a common operating system for research while allowing the specific research method to change according to the question.

# 3. Research Philosophy

## What thing

**Research philosophy**

## Choice

**Evidence-driven and hypothesis-driven by default, with exploratory research when appropriate.**

## What it means

Research should begin with a desire to reduce uncertainty rather than simply collect information.

Whenever possible, the team converts an assumption into a testable hypothesis.

For example:

Instead of:

> "Research whether customers like our product."

The team asks:

> "We believe customers abandon the product because onboarding takes too long. What evidence would support or disprove this?"

This produces a much sharper investigation.

The research team identifies:

* What is known
* What is unknown
* What is assumed
* What evidence exists
* What evidence is missing
* What evidence would change the conclusion
* How confident the company should be

## Why this was selected

A company does not conduct research merely to accumulate information.

It conducts research because uncertainty affects decisions.

Evidence-driven research therefore keeps research connected to reality.

Hypothesis-driven thinking also prevents researchers from unconsciously searching only for information that confirms what people already believe.

## Tradeoff

Not every useful research question can be expressed as a clean hypothesis.

Some discoveries begin with:

> "We don't know what is happening."

Forcing these investigations into rigid hypotheses can create artificial assumptions.

Therefore:

**Hypothesis-driven when possible. Exploratory when necessary.**

# 4. Research Strategy

## What thing

**Research strategy**

## Choice

**Decision-led and problem-led research**

## What it means

The research team starts by identifying the decision or problem that requires better information.

The starting question is:

> "What should the company know in order to make a better decision?"

Not:

> "What research can we perform?"

For example:

### Product

Decision:

> Should Feature X be built?

Research:

> Determine whether the problem is significant, who experiences it, how frequently it occurs, what alternatives exist, and whether the proposed solution addresses it.

### Engineering

Decision:

> Should the organization migrate to technology X?

Research:

> Compare technical capability, cost, reliability, performance, security, migration complexity and long-term implications.

### Finance

Decision:

> Should the company make a particular investment?

Research:

> Evaluate expected returns, downside risk, alternatives, assumptions and sensitivity to changing conditions.

## Why this was selected

It prevents research from becoming detached from business reality.

A beautifully written 100-page report is useless if nobody knows what decision it is supposed to influence.

## Tradeoff

Decision-led research can underfund curiosity-driven research.

Therefore the company should maintain a separate portion of its research portfolio for exploratory and long-term work.

# 5. Organizational Structure

## What thing

**Research organizational model**

## Choice

**Centralized core + federated domain expertise**

## What it means

The company has a central Research & Intelligence function responsible for:

* Research standards
* Research methodology
* Research quality
* Research operations
* Core researchers
* Research infrastructure
* Knowledge management
* Intelligence
* Research governance

However, domain specialists can come from or work closely with other functions.

For example:

```text
Central Research
      │
      ├── Market Researcher
      ├── Research Analyst
      ├── Data Scientist
      └── Research Engineer
             │
             ├──── Product Specialist
             ├──── Security Specialist
             ├──── Engineering Specialist
             ├──── Finance Specialist
             ├──── Legal Specialist
             └──── Operations Specialist
```

The specialists contribute domain knowledge when required.

## Why this was selected

A completely centralized model creates distance from the departments.

A completely decentralized model creates:

* Duplicated research
* Different standards
* Conflicting conclusions
* Fragmented knowledge
* Poor reuse
* Methodological inconsistency

The hybrid model gives the company:

**Central consistency + local expertise.**

## Tradeoff

The model requires coordination.

There will be some overhead from:

* Assigning specialists
* Coordinating projects
* Maintaining standards
* Resolving ownership questions

This is accepted because the alternative is organizational fragmentation.

**Research Core**

## Main responsibilities

The Research Core handles:

* Research design
* Problem decomposition
* Evidence gathering
* Literature/document research
* Interviews
* Surveys
* Analysis
* Synthesis
* Experiment design
* Research writing

## Why

This creates a reusable research capability instead of forcing every department to maintain its own complete research organization.

## Tradeoff

# 7. Domain Specialist Model

## What thing

**Domain specialization**

## Choice

Maintain specialists that can be attached to research projects when required.

Possible domains include:

* Market
* Customer
* UX
* Product
* Technology
* Engineering
* Security
* AI/ML
* Data
* Finance
* Legal
* Regulatory
* Operations
* Supply chain
* Human resources
* Industry/scientific domains

## How it works

A research project creates a temporary cross-functional research group.

Example:

```text
Research Lead
     │
     ├── Market Researcher
     ├── UX Researcher
     ├── Data Scientist
     ├── Security Specialist
     └── Product Specialist
```

The exact composition depends on the question.

## Why

Research problems rarely respect departmental boundaries.

A product decision may simultaneously involve:

* Customer behavior
* Market economics
* Security
* Technology
* UX
* Finance

A flexible specialist pool handles this better than rigid departments.

## Tradeoff

Specialists may have competing priorities.

This requires clear project ownership and allocation mechanisms.

# 8. Research Methodology

## What thing

**Research methodology**

## Choice

**Mixed-method research**

## What it means

The organization does not declare one universal method such as qualitative research, quantitative research, Design Thinking, Lean Startup, or scientific experimentation.

Instead:

> **The research question determines the appropriate method.**

Possible methods include:

### Qualitative

* Interviews
* Observation
* Focus groups
* Contextual inquiry
* Expert interviews
* Usability studies

### Quantitative

* Surveys
* Statistical analysis
* Behavioral analysis
* Experiments
* Forecasting
* Benchmarking

### Secondary research

* Academic literature
* Technical documentation
* Industry reports
* Standards
* Patents
* Regulations
* Public datasets
* Existing company data
* Competitor information

### Experimental research

* Controlled experiments
* Prototypes
* Simulations
* Technical benchmarks
* Pilot programs
* A/B tests

## Why

Different questions require different evidence.

For example:

> "Why do users struggle?"

Qualitative research may be strongest.

> "How many users experience this?"

Quantitative research may be stronger.

> "Does changing X cause Y?"

Experimentation may be stronger.

> "What technologies already solve this?"

Secondary technical research may be sufficient.

## Tradeoff

Mixed-method research requires more expertise and can increase research time.

The benefit is much greater methodological flexibility.

# 9. Secondary Research

## What thing

**Secondary research**

## Choice

**Use existing evidence before generating new evidence.**

## How it works

A research project first searches:

1. Internal company knowledge
2. Existing company data
3. Previous research
4. Academic literature
5. Technical documentation
6. Standards
7. Regulations
8. Industry research
9. Public datasets
10. Competitor information

Only after determining what is already known does the team decide what additional research is required.

## Why

This prevents duplicated effort.

Without this rule, multiple departments may unknowingly research the same problem.

## Tradeoff

Existing information can be:

* Outdated
* Biased
* Incomplete
* Based on different assumptions

Therefore secondary research should not automatically be treated as truth.

It is evidence, not certainty.

---

# 10. Primary Research

## What thing

**Primary research**

## Choice

**Use targeted primary research when existing evidence cannot adequately answer the question.**

## Methods

* Interviews
* Surveys
* Observational studies
* User testing
* Field studies
* Experiments
* Technical testing
* Prototyping
* Pilot programs
* Expert interviews

## How it works

The team identifies a specific information gap.

It then designs the smallest research activity capable of producing credible evidence about that gap.

## Why

Primary research provides evidence directly relevant to the company's particular situation.

## Tradeoff

It can be:

* Expensive
* Slow
* Difficult to scale
* Subject to sampling bias
* Subject to researcher bias

Therefore primary research should be **targeted rather than automatic**.

---

# 11. Experimentation

## What thing

**Experimental research**

## Choice

**Use experimentation aggressively when uncertainty is high and the question is experimentally testable.**

## How it works

The basic cycle is:

```text
Hypothesis
     ↓
Smallest credible test
     ↓
Measure
     ↓
Analyze
     ↓
Decision
```

Instead of building an entire product and hoping it works, the company attempts to test its riskiest assumption first.

For example:

```text
Idea
 ↓
Prototype
 ↓
Small user test
 ↓
Evidence
 ↓
Improve / Build / Abandon
```

## Why

Experiments can prevent expensive mistakes.

They turn arguments into measurable evidence.

## Tradeoff

Some things cannot be cheaply experimented with.

Examples include:

* Major infrastructure
* Large physical systems
* Certain regulated activities
* Long-horizon investments
* Some scientific questions

Experimentation is therefore a powerful method, not the entire research system.

---

# 12. Design Sprints

## What thing

**Design Sprint**

## Choice

Use Design Sprints as a **specialized research and validation technique**, not as the company's universal research methodology.

## How it works

A multidisciplinary team rapidly:

1. Understands the problem
2. Defines the challenge
3. Generates solutions
4. Selects an approach
5. Creates a prototype
6. Tests it with users

## Best use cases

* Product ideas
* UX problems
* New workflows
* Uncertain customer problems
* Prototype validation

## Why

It compresses lengthy discussion into rapid learning.

## Tradeoff

It is poorly suited to:

* Deep scientific research
* Complex financial research
* Long-term technology investigation
* Regulatory analysis
* Large statistical studies

Therefore it remains one tool inside the larger research system.

# 13. Research Modes

The research organization should support several modes.

## Exploratory Research

### Purpose

Discover what is happening when the company does not yet understand the problem.

### Example

> "We are seeing unexpected customer behavior. We don't know why."

### Tradeoff

Broad and potentially slow.

---

## Diagnostic Research

### Purpose

Determine the cause of an existing problem.

### Example

> "System reliability declined. What caused it?"

### Tradeoff

Can become overly focused on existing assumptions.

---

## Evaluative Research

### Purpose

Determine whether something works.

### Example

> "Did the new onboarding process improve conversion?"

### Tradeoff

Requires good measurement design.

---

## Comparative Research

### Purpose

Determine which option performs better.

### Example

> "Which architecture is better for our requirements?"

### Tradeoff

Results depend heavily on how comparison criteria are chosen.

---

## Predictive Research

### Purpose

Estimate what is likely to happen.

### Example

> "What demand should we expect next year?"

### Tradeoff

Predictions become less reliable as uncertainty and time horizon increase.

---

## Experimental Research

### Purpose

Determine whether changing one factor produces a measurable effect.

### Tradeoff

Controlled experimentation isn't always possible.

---

## Strategic Research

### Purpose

Support major long-term decisions.

### Examples

* Market entry
* Acquisition
* Technology investment
* Business-model changes

### Tradeoff

Strategic research often deals with incomplete information.

---

## Intelligence Research

### Purpose

Detect changes in the company's external environment.

### Examples

* Competitor moves
* Technology changes
* Regulatory developments
* Market shifts
* Threat changes

### Tradeoff

Continuous monitoring produces significant noise unless carefully filtered.

* Statistical analysis

# 19. Triangulation

## What thing

**Evidence triangulation**

## Choice

Use multiple independent evidence sources for important conclusions.

## How it works

For example:

```text
Customer Interviews
        +
Product Analytics
        +
Competitor Research
        +
Experiment
        ↓
Combined Evidence
        ↓
Conclusion
```

If multiple independent sources point in the same direction, confidence increases.

If they disagree, the disagreement itself becomes a research finding.

## Why

Every research method has weaknesses.

Triangulation reduces dependence on one source or methodology.

## Tradeoff

It takes more time.

Not every small decision requires extensive triangulation.

The depth should therefore match the importance and uncertainty of the decision.

# 20. Confidence and Uncertainty

## What thing

**Research confidence model**

## Choice

Every significant conclusion should communicate uncertainty explicitly.

A research result should distinguish:

* Finding
* Evidence
* Confidence
* Assumptions
* Limitations
* Unknowns
* Contradictory evidence

For example:

```text
Finding:
Customers experience onboarding friction.

Evidence:
8/10 interviews identified the issue.
Analytics show a 31% abandonment point.

Confidence:
High

Remaining uncertainty:
Exact cause of abandonment.

Recommendation:
Run controlled onboarding experiment.
```

## Why

Research cannot eliminate uncertainty.

Its job is to **reduce uncertainty enough to make better decisions**.

## Tradeoff

Executives sometimes want simple answers:

> Yes.

> No.

Reality is often:

> High confidence.

> Medium confidence.

> Low confidence.

The latter is more honest and more useful.

# 21. Research Portfolio

## What thing

**Research portfolio strategy**

## Choice

Maintain three broad categories:

### Incremental

Research that improves existing products, processes or capabilities.

### Adjacent

Research into nearby markets, technologies, products or capabilities.

### Exploratory

Research into uncertain, long-term possibilities.

```text
                 RESEARCH PORTFOLIO
                        │
          ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
          ▼             ▼             ▼
     Incremental     Adjacent     Exploratory
       Present       Near future    Long future
```

## Why

A company that only researches current problems becomes vulnerable to future changes.

A company that only researches speculative ideas may waste resources.

The portfolio balances both.

## Tradeoff

Exploratory research has uncertain returns.

That uncertainty is accepted deliberately.

## What thing

**Research Operations — Research Ops**

## Choice

Create a dedicated operational capability responsible for making research repeatable, organized and scalable.

## Responsibilities

* Research standards
* Research templates
* Research repository
* Participant management
* Data management
* Research tooling
* Documentation
* Quality control
* Research scheduling
* Research governance
* Methodology library
* Reproducibility
* Knowledge management

## Why

Without Research Ops, researchers spend excessive time managing administrative work.

More importantly, research becomes inconsistent.

## Tradeoff

Research Ops itself creates overhead.

But at organizational scale, that overhead is considerably smaller than the cost of fragmented research.

# 23. Institutional Knowledge Management

## What thing

**Research knowledge repository**

## Choice

Every significant research project should become part of the company's institutional memory.

The repository should preserve:

* Research question
* Decision
* Method
* Raw evidence where appropriate
* Analysis
* Findings
* Confidence
* Limitations
* Recommendation
* Outcome
* Follow-up research

## Why

A company should not repeatedly ask questions it has already answered.

Research should accumulate into organizational knowledge.

## Tradeoff

Repositories become useless if poorly maintained.

Therefore Research Ops must maintain:

* Searchability
* Metadata
* Ownership
* Versioning
* Archival
* Quality standards

## What thing

**Standard research lifecycle**

## Choice

Use one universal lifecycle across departments.

```text
1. QUESTION
      ↓
2. FRAME
      ↓
3. HYPOTHESIS / EXPLORATION
      ↓
4. PLAN
      ↓
5. EXISTING EVIDENCE
      ↓
6. METHOD SELECTION
      ↓
7. DATA / EVIDENCE COLLECTION
      ↓
8. VERIFICATION
      ↓
9. ANALYSIS
      ↓
10. SYNTHESIS
      ↓
11. RECOMMENDATION
      ↓
12. DECISION
      ↓
13. ACTION
      ↓
14. MEASUREMENT
      ↓
```

Different research projects can vary enormously, but having a common lifecycle makes the organization understandable.

The process scales in depth rather than changing completely.

# 25. Research Question

## What thing

**Question definition**

## Choice

Every research project begins with an explicit question.

The question should describe what needs to be learned.

Bad:

> "Research AI."

Better:

> "Which locally deployable AI architectures can meet our latency, privacy and cost requirements?"

The second question is actionable.

## Why

Poor questions produce unfocused research.

Good questions define the boundaries of investigation.

---

# 26. Research Hypothesis

## What thing

**Hypothesis**

## Choice

Use hypotheses whenever the problem is sufficiently understood.

A hypothesis is a proposition that evidence can support or challenge.

Example:

> "Reducing onboarding from five steps to three will increase activation."

The research then determines whether evidence supports that proposition.

## Tradeoff

A premature hypothesis can constrain discovery.

Therefore exploratory research should precede hypothesis-driven work when the problem itself is poorly understood.

# 29. Research Prioritization

## What thing

**Research backlog**

## Choice

Research requests are prioritized based on factors such as:

* Business impact
* Risk
* Uncertainty
* Strategic importance
* Urgency
* Cost of being wrong
* Decision deadline
* Research effort

A useful conceptual model is:

> **Priority increases when impact and uncertainty are high, particularly when the cost of making the wrong decision is high.**

## Why

Research capacity is finite.

Not every question deserves a six-week investigation.

# 32. Relationship Between Research and Departments

## What thing

**Research ownership model**

## Choice

> **Research owns the investigation. The requesting department owns the decision.**

For example:

Product asks:

> "Should we build Feature X?"

Research determines:

> What evidence exists and what does it suggest?

Product ultimately decides:

> Build / modify / postpone / reject.

Similarly:

* Engineering owns engineering decisions.
* Finance owns financial decisions.
* Security owns security decisions.
* Marketing owns marketing decisions.
* Leadership owns strategic decisions.

## Why

Research should be an independent source of evidence rather than another layer of management.

# 35. Research Quality Control

## What thing

**Research quality assurance**

## Choice

Important research should be reviewed for:

* Methodological correctness
* Source quality
* Evidence sufficiency
* Bias
* Statistical validity where applicable
* Reproducibility
* Logical consistency
* Alternative explanations
* Contradictory evidence
* Uncertainty

## Why

Research itself can be wrong.

A research department without quality control simply creates more sophisticated-looking mistakes.

## Tradeoff

Review takes time.

The review depth should therefore scale with decision importance.

# 36. Research Ethics and Governance

## What thing

**Research governance**

## Choice

Use proportional governance.

Low-risk research should remain lightweight.

High-risk research receives stronger controls.

Governance may address:

* Privacy
* Consent
* Sensitive information
* Data handling
* Conflicts of interest
* Research integrity
* Regulatory requirements
* Security
* Participant protection

## Why

Different research activities carry different risks.

A universal company model cannot treat every project identically.

## Tradeoff

Too much governance slows research.

Too little governance can create serious organizational risk.

The correct principle is:

> **Minimum necessary governance, proportional to risk.**

# 38. Automation Philosophy

## What thing

**Research automation**

## Choice

Automate repetitive work while keeping important judgment human-controlled.

Good automation targets:

* Data collection
* Data cleaning
* Report generation
* Citation management
* Dataset processing
* Repeated analysis
* Monitoring
* Notifications
* Repository indexing
* Research templates
* Experiment execution

Human researchers should remain responsible for:

* Research questions
* Interpretation
* Methodological judgment
* Critical evaluation
* Ethical decisions
* Final conclusions
* Strategic recommendations

## Why

Automation increases research throughput without requiring proportional increases in headcount.

## Tradeoff

Automation can amplify errors.

A bad automated process can produce thousands of bad results quickly.

Therefore automated research pipelines need validation and monitoring.

# 39. Decision Threshold

## What thing

**Research stopping criteria**

## Choice

Research should stop when it has produced enough evidence to support the required decision with an acceptable level of confidence.

Research should not continue indefinitely simply because more information could theoretically be collected.

The stopping question is:

> "Would additional research materially change the decision?"

If the answer is no, the research should normally stop.

## Why

Perfect knowledge is impossible.

The objective is sufficient decision quality, not infinite information.

## Tradeoff

Stopping too early creates risk.

Continuing too long creates wasted resources.

The research lead must balance both.

# 40. Research-to-Decision Loop

Research is not finished when the report is delivered.

The complete loop is:

```text
Research
   ↓
Recommendation
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Action
   ↓
Real-world result
   ↓
Measurement
   ↓
Compare prediction vs reality
   ↓
Update knowledge
```

This is critical.

The company should eventually learn:

> Did our research actually improve our decisions?

```text
        │

        ├── Qualitative when useful
```

# 43. Why This Combination Is the Best Universal Default

No single existing methodology is designed to handle every type of company question.

A market researcher, security researcher, scientist, UX researcher and financial analyst cannot realistically perform identical research.

But they **can share the same operating philosophy and lifecycle**.

The selected architecture therefore separates:

### What remains universal

* Research begins with a question
* Decisions are explicitly identified
* Existing evidence is checked first
* Evidence is evaluated critically
* Methods are selected deliberately
* Important conclusions communicate uncertainty
* Research is documented
* Results feed decisions
* Outcomes are measured
* Knowledge is retained

### What remains flexible

* Exact research methodology
* Data sources
* Analysis techniques
* Team composition
* Research duration
* Experimental design
* Domain expertise
* Depth of investigation

This separation is what makes the model scalable.

# 45. The Model in One Sentence

The entire system can ultimately be reduced to this:

> **A centralized Research & Intelligence function provides a common evidence-driven operating system, draws on federated domain expertise, selects the appropriate research methodology for each question, prioritizes decisions according to impact and uncertainty, generates or gathers evidence, analyzes and triangulates it, communicates confidence and limitations, delivers recommendations to the responsible department, measures the resulting outcome, and permanently captures what was learned.**

That is the **Universal Company Research & Intelligence Model**.

It is deliberately not a rigid research methodology.
