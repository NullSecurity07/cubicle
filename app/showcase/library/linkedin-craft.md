# LinkedIn craft

*Written for this app from what the MIT-licensed "linkedin-skills" by Sergey Bulaev teaches,
https://github.com/sergebulaev/linkedin-skills (post writer, comment drafter, reply handler, humanizer, hook
extractor, content planner, profile optimizer, interviewer). Distilled and rewritten in our own words; none of their
text is copied. The reach figures they cite come from third-party studies and vendors, not from LinkedIn, so the rules
below are directions, not guarantees.*

---

## 1. The first line and "see more"

- LinkedIn cuts a post after about 140 characters on a phone and about 210 on a laptop, then shows "…see more".
  Write for the phone: the first line alone has to earn the tap.
- Line one is a statement or a number. Never a question, never "Here's what…", never "Stop X, start Y".
- A specific number in the first line beats a round one: $873 beats "almost $900", 11 months beats "a long time".
- A real failure, dated, in the first three lines beats a polished win. State it flat, without "let me be honest".
- A tap on "see more" followed by leaving at once counts against the post. The rest must pay off the first line.

## 2. Hook patterns

Pick one per post. Two hooks in one post weaken both.

1. **Odd-precision ledger** — "We spent $4,730 on servers in March. $3,100 of it was one forgotten test job."
2. **Dated reversal** — "In January I told our team we would never build X. We shipped it on 12 June."
3. **Controlled comparison** — "Same product, same week, two landing pages. One headline got 4x the sign-ups."
4. **Category obituary** — "Cold email as we knew it is over. Here is what killed it:" followed by the evidence.
5. **Contrarian with receipts** — a claim most peers would argue with, then two or three dated facts behind it.
6. **Cold open** — start inside the moment: "The call ended at 11:40 and the client had cancelled."
7. **Give-away reversal** — "I charged $2,000 for this checklist last year. Here it is free."
8. **Before and after** — "A year ago: 3 users, all friends. Today: 412, and I know none of them."

## 3. Post shapes

- **Story**: the moment (one line) → what was at stake → what happened, with one number and one name → what changed
  in how you work → a closing question about the reader's own version.
- **List**: a claim → 3 to 7 items, each one line with a concrete detail → which one mattered most and why.
  A list people save is a checklist or a template.
- **Contrarian**: the common belief, stated fairly → why you think it is wrong → the evidence you have seen → where
  you might be wrong → a question that invites people who disagree.
- **Lesson**: what you did → what broke, dated → what you do now → the one rule you would give someone starting today.
- **Question**: a real situation with numbers → the two options you are weighing → ask which the reader would pick
  and why. The question goes at the end, never in line one.

Close with a specific question ("What's the worst migration you've had to roll back?"), not "Thoughts?". A one-line
P.S. with a real follow-up helps. No "Agree? Comment below!": LinkedIn pushes that kind of bait down.

## 4. Length and line breaks

- Most posts: 900 to 1,300 characters. Long posts (1,500 to 1,900) work only with a story that pays off.
- One or two sentences per paragraph, a blank line between ideas. Not one sentence per line for the whole post.
- At most one contrast ("not X but Y") and one list of three per post.
- No more than two single-word or fragment paragraphs in a post.
- Hashtags: none, or one to three niche ones at the very end.

## 5. What the feed rewards (as reported, not confirmed by LinkedIn)

- **Dwell time**: people stopping to read, around 30 to 60 seconds, counts for more than a like.
- **Early comments**: the first hour decides most of a post's reach. Reply to every real comment within that hour;
  a back-and-forth thread counts more than many one-word reactions.
- **Saves** count for more than likes; checklists and templates get saved.
- **No external links in the body**: a link in the post cuts reach sharply. Put it in the first comment and say
  "link in the first comment".
- **Cadence**: three to five posts a week; two posts in one day compete with each other. Tuesday to Thursday
  mornings in the audience's time zone are best for work topics; weekends are weak.
- **Editing**: fix typos after the first hour; rewriting a large part of a fresh post resets it.
- **Pods and automation** (groups that comment on each other's posts on cue, bots, identical replies) are detected
  and punished. Every comment and reply here is written for one post and sent by hand.

## 6. Comments on other people's posts

A good comment gets the author to reply. It adds one thing the post did not have.

- 200 to 350 characters, one or two short paragraphs, no hashtags, no link, no mention of your product by name.
- Shapes that work: the missing piece ("the argument misses one thing: …"), answering the author's closing question
  with a real example, a data point from your own work, a practitioner's "when X happens, Y follows", agree with one
  point and push back on another with a reason, a sharper version of the author's question.
- Never generic praise: "Great post!", "This.", "100%", "So true" are ignored and make you look like a bot.
- Skip sponsored posts and generic listicles.
- Ten to twenty thoughtful comments a day on peers' posts bring more people to your profile than posting more.

## 7. Replies on your own posts

- LinkedIn keeps only two levels: a reply to a reply is filed under the top comment. Start the reply with the
  person's name so they know it is to them.
- 150 to 300 characters. Answer their question directly with one real detail; if they pushed back, concede what is
  right and sharpen what is not; or share what happened to you.
- React to the comment you answer before you reply.
- No canned "Thanks!": reply with something, or not at all.
- Replies go out one at a time, by hand. A dozen landing in a minute looks automated.
- Past three days, a direct message is often better than a late reply.

## 8. Humanizer: what to cut and what to write instead

Read every draft for these before it is shown. One marker in a paragraph is fine; three means rewrite the paragraph.

| Cut | Write instead |
|---|---|
| delve, dive into | look at, go through |
| game-changer, revolutionary | say what changed, with the number |
| unlock, unleash | get, open, start |
| leverage, harness | use |
| "In today's fast-paced world…" | start with the fact |
| a rhetorical question as the opener | the number or the moment |
| three adjectives in a row ("simple, powerful, intuitive") | one adjective with proof |
| em-dash chains (more than one per 100 words) | a comma, a colon, or two sentences |
| emoji bullet walls (🚀 ✅ 💡 on every line) | plain lines, at most one emoji a post |
| "The result?", "Plot twist:", "Here's the thing" | say the result |
| "No X. No Y. Just Z." | one plain sentence |
| "Let me be honest", "real talk" | the dated fact, stated flat |
| "It's not X, it's Y" more than once | keep one contrast |
| passionate, driven, results-oriented, thought leader | what you did, with a number |

Add, when the founder has given them: one odd-precision number with what it refers to, one named company, person or
tool, one first-person detail (something seen, said or done), one date. Never invent any of them: ask.

Do not scrub a clean draft for the sake of it; two or three touches is normal. Keep the founder's own quirks.

## 9. Profile

- **Headline** (up to 220 characters): what you do | who you help | the result, e.g. "I build invoicing for
  freelance designers | 412 studios paid on time last month".
- **About** (200 to 300 words, first person, "I help…", never "Jane is a passionate…"). The first 265 characters
  show before "see more". Order: a hook with a number → what you do and for whom → one or two wins with numbers → the
  problem you solve and how → one human line → keywords people search for → what to do next.
- **Featured**: three items that match the goal (customers: a case study and a way to book a call; hiring: best
  work; authority: best posts).
- Experience lines are an action and a number. Claim a clean profile address.

## 10. Story bank: ten questions to ask the founder

Ask one at a time. Press a soft answer once ("by how much?", "which month?"), then move on.

1. What have you been working on that you cannot stop thinking about?
2. When did the problem your product solves last happen to you or a customer? Walk me through that day.
3. What did you believe a year ago that you no longer believe, and what did it cost to find out?
4. What do you think is true that most people in your field would argue with?
5. What is the most expensive mistake you have made building this, in money or months?
6. Which customer conversation changed what you built? What did they say, word for word?
7. What number about your product are you proudest of, and which one worries you?
8. Which three stories do you already tell in person, because they always land?
9. Who helped you get here, and what exactly did they do?
10. What can never be named in public: which clients, people, numbers or subjects stay out?
