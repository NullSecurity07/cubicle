# Weekly metrics that tell the truth

*How a small company picks its numbers and reads them every week. Written for this app in our own words from two YC
Startup School talks, Tom Blomfield's "Key Startup Metrics"
(https://www.ycombinator.com/library/KR-key-startup-metrics) and Divya Bhat's "Setting KPIs and goals"
(https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iu-setting-kpis-and-goals), with Amplitude's North Star guide
(https://amplitude.com/blog/product-north-star-metric) and Lenny Rachitsky's retention benchmarks
(https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1277620704146423809.html).*

## Four or five, written down

Pick four or five metrics before you launch, not fifty. The simplest tool that works is fine. Write down exactly what
each one means, including its time window, and agree it with everyone. Arguing about definitions wastes more time
than having no metrics. Never change a definition because the number disappointed you: switching from weekly to
monthly actives to look better only fools you.

## Vanity numbers

Big numbers that do not track the health of the company: page views, unique visitors, gross merchandise value,
gross transaction value. Blomfield tells of a company whose transaction value grew fast while revenue sat flat,
because it was paying customers rebates to transact. If a number can rise while the business stalls, it is not the
primary KPI.

## One primary KPI

One number decides whether the week went well. After launch, for most companies, it is revenue growth. Exceptions
exist (a marketplace may use sign-ups or volume; an enterprise company with long sales cycles may count signed
letters of intent), but you should be able to say why. Secondary KPIs exist to keep the primary honest or to give early
warning. Do not optimise two hard things at once.

## A North Star before revenue

Before you charge, pick one number that shows customers getting real value, that reflects your strategy, and that
leads future revenue. Amplitude calls this the North Star and suggests three to five input metrics under it
(breadth, depth, frequency, efficiency). Its examples: trial accounts with three or more users active in week one;
early Facebook's users adding seven friends in ten days.

## The weekly row

Each week, the same row: every metric, this week against last, the change in percent. Always include revenue, net
burn (monthly costs minus revenue) and runway (cash divided by net burn). Blomfield: if those three are not at the top
of an update, readers assume something is being hidden. One founder he admired sent ten monthly updates with zero
revenue at the top, and it kept her honest.

## The weekly goal

Set a goal for the primary KPI every week, and set it two ways: top-down, backing into the weekly rate a milestone
needs; and bottom-up, from what this week's work could add. Paul Graham's figures, as Bhat cites them: 5 to 7% growth
a week is good for a YC company, 10% is exceptional.

## The biggest blocker

Each week name the one thing most holding the primary KPI back and work only on that. Retro at the end of the week:
did you predict impact well, and did you finish what you planned?

## Retention, stacked

Group customers by the month they joined and track how many are still active or paying one, two, three and six months
later. Stacked, good cohorts build a layer cake that keeps paying; bad ones empty out and you refill a leaking bucket.
What matters most is that the curve flattens, even at a low level. For B2B, net dollar retention above 100% means
cohorts grow on their own; Blomfield suggests 125 to 150% for early B2B SaaS and 110 to 120% for mature companies.

## Benchmarks, with their years

Six-month user retention, good and great, from Lenny Rachitsky's 2020 survey of 20+ operators and investors: consumer
social 25% and 45%; consumer transactional 30% and 50%; consumer subscription 40% and 70%; SMB and mid-market SaaS
60% and 80%; enterprise SaaS 70% and 90%. Benchmarks are old and averaged: use them to ask questions, not to grade.
