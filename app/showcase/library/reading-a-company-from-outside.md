# Reading a company from outside

*How to learn who you are about to take money from, sell to or compete with, before the meeting, using only what is
public. Written for this app in our own words. The registries and sites named below are pointers to where facts are
kept; nothing from them is copied here.*

---

## The rule

Every line you write about another company ends with a link to where you read it, and the date it was published or
the date you read it. A line without a link is an opinion, and it does not go in the Profile.

"Not found" is a result. It tells the founder where you looked and that the answer was not there, which is worth
knowing before a meeting: it is a question to ask them. A guessed number is worse than no number, because it will be
repeated in the room as if it were true.

## Where facts live

Start at the company's own site, then go to the places that keep records the company does not control.

| Where | What it holds | How much to trust it |
|---|---|---|
| The company's site | What they say they do, pricing page, team page, blog, changelog, customer logos | Their own words: good for what they claim, not for whether it is true |
| Companies House (UK) | Incorporation date, registered address, officers and when they joined or left, people with significant control, filed accounts, share allotments | A record. Filings are dated; small companies file short accounts |
| SEC EDGAR (US) | Public companies' annual and quarterly reports and 8-K events; Form D notices when a private company raises under an exemption | A record. A Form D gives the amount sold and the date, not the valuation |
| OpenCorporates | One search across many national registers, with links back to each | An index. Follow the link to the register before you cite it |
| EU national registers | Germany's Handelsregister, France's annuaire des entreprises, the Netherlands' KVK and others; the EU e-Justice portal links them | Records, often in the local language; some charge for full extracts |
| Other registers | India's MCA, Singapore's ACRA, Australia's ASIC and so on: search "<country> company register" | Records; fees and depth vary |
| Crunchbase (public page) | Funding rounds, investors, founders, headcount range | Partly self-reported and often stale. Cite the announcement it points to, not Crunchbase alone |
| LinkedIn company page | Headcount range, where people work, recent hires and posts | Self-reported by staff; good for trends, weak for exact numbers |
| G2, Capterra and similar | Customer reviews, the sizes of companies that buy, what they complain about | Real buyers but a selected sample; read the dated negatives |
| Job boards and their careers page | What they are hiring for, where, how senior | A strong signal of where they are going next |
| GitHub | For tech companies: what they ship, how often, how many outside contributors | A record of work; a quiet repo can mean a private move, not a stop |
| App stores | Ratings, review dates, release notes, how often they ship | Dated and public |
| Wayback Machine | What the site said before: old pricing, old team pages, removed claims | A record of what was published, with the capture date |
| Press | Funding announcements, launches, interviews | A dated claim. A funding story is the company's number unless a filing agrees |

## How to date a fact

Every fact has two dates: when it happened and when it was said. Write the one that matters for the claim, and say
which. "Raised a 4M seed (announced 2025-03-11, link)" is a fact. "Raised a 4M seed" is not, because the founder does
not know whether it was last month or four years ago.

When a page shows no date, look for one in the URL, the page's metadata, the first Wayback capture, or the comments.
If none can be found, write "undated" and treat it as old.

Recent news means the last 90 days from today. Anything older is history, not news.

## A rumour or a record

A record is something the company had to file, or something written at the time by someone who could see it: a
registry filing, a court record, a regulator's notice, the company's own dated announcement. A rumour is anything
passed on: "sources say", a forum post, a number in a listicle, a Crunchbase field with no source behind it.

Rumours may go in the Profile when they are marked as rumours and linked. Two rumours that agree are still rumours if
one copied the other; look for the first place it was said. When a record and a rumour disagree, the record wins, and
the gap is often worth a question in the meeting.

## Three tells for an investor

1. **Fund age and dry powder.** Find the fund they would invest from, when it closed and how big it is. Funds usually
   make new investments in their first three or four years and keep the rest for follow-ons. A fund near the end of
   that window may take the meeting and still not write a new cheque. Compare their usual cheque to the ask: a cheque
   far outside their range is a no before it starts.
2. **Follow-on rate.** Look at their portfolio's later rounds. Do they come back in, or does someone else always
   lead? An investor who never follows on leaves the founder alone at the next raise. Portfolio pages plus the
   companies' own funding announcements are enough to count.
3. **How founders describe them.** Read what founders they backed say in public: posts, podcasts, threads, and
   especially founders of companies that did not work out. Ask the founder to call two of those people. Slow
   decisions, late changes to terms and pressure to sell early show up here and nowhere else.

Also check portfolio conflicts. An investor holding a direct competitor may still take the meeting, and learn a lot.

## Tells for a customer

1. **Payment terms.** Large buyers often pay 60 or 90 days after the invoice, and some pay later. Look for their
   supplier terms, procurement pages or public complaints from vendors. A long gap is cash the founder must carry.
2. **Procurement.** Does a purchase need a security review, a vendor registration, a legal redline, a paid pilot
   first? Their supplier page often says. Count the steps before the first invoice.
3. **The incumbent vendor.** Who do they buy from now? Job posts that name tools, case studies on a vendor's site and
   conference talks by their staff will say. Replacing an incumbent is a different sale from filling a gap.

Churn signals: layoffs, a new leader in the buying team, a hiring freeze, a budget cut in the news.

## Tells for a competitor

1. **Pricing page diffs.** Compare today's pricing page with Wayback captures from 3, 6 and 12 months ago. A new
   cheaper tier, a removed free plan or a jump to "contact us" says where they are going.
2. **Hiring.** What roles, in which cities, how senior. A run of enterprise sales hires means they are moving up
   market; a burst of engineers in one area means a product is coming.
3. **Changelog cadence.** How often they ship, and what. A changelog that slowed down, or turned to integrations and
   admin features, is a company changing whom it builds for.

## Before the meeting

The founder should walk in knowing: what they do in one line, how old and how big they are, what they raised and
from whom (with the filing if there is one), what changed in the last 90 days, who will be in the room and what each
has said in public, the three risks in this relationship, and three questions only this meeting can answer.
