# Hiring against a scorecard

*How a small company decides whom to hire. Written for this app in our own words from Who: The A Method for Hiring
by Geoff Smart and Randy Street (publisher excerpt:
https://geoffsmart.com/books/who-the-a-method-for-hiring/who-the-book-excerpt/), with the summaries by Nat Eliason
(https://www.nateliason.com/notes/who-geoff-smart) and Jaap Grolleman
(https://jaapgrolleman.com/who-hiring-summary/).*

## Why it matters

The book's excerpt puts the average cost of a hiring mistake at about fifteen times the person's base salary, once
lost time and output are counted, and says managers' hires work out about half the time. Its method has four steps:
scorecard, source, select, sell.

## Mission

One or two sentences on why the job exists. If you cannot write it, you are not ready to hire.

## Outcomes, not duties

Three to eight results the person must achieve, ranked, with numbers and dates where possible. "Grow new revenue from
mid-market accounts to $40k a month by month six," not "manage the sales pipeline." Set them high but reachable.

## Competencies

How the person must work to get those results here: for example follow-through, attention to detail, persistence,
initiative. Write what each means in your company, so two interviewers mean the same thing.

## Sourcing starts with people you know

The most reliable source is referrals from your own network and your team's. Ask regularly, not only when a role is
open.

## The screening call

Fifteen to thirty minutes, four questions: what are your career goals; what are you really good at; what are you not
good at or not interested in; and who were your last bosses and how will they rate you when we talk to them. The last
question matters: people answer more honestly when they expect you to check.

## The career walk-through

Go through each past job in order: what were you hired to do; what are you proud of; what went wrong; who did you
work with and what was your boss like; why did you leave. Patterns across jobs tell you more than any single answer.

## References that tell the truth

Talk to past bosses the candidate named. Ask how you worked together, their greatest strengths, what they needed to
improve, how they would rate them from one to ten, and about something the candidate told you. Anything under eight
deserves a follow-up question.

## Rating against the scorecard

Rate every outcome and competency A, B or C, and write the evidence next to each rating. No evidence, no rating.

## Selling

The people you want have options. Talk to what matters to them: whether the role fits their goals, their family's
situation, freedom to do the work their way, the money, and whether it will be fun.
