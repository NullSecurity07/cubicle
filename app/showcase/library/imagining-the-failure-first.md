# Imagining the failure first

*A way to find a plan's weak points before you commit. Written for this app in our own words from Gary Klein's
"Performing a Project Premortem", Harvard Business Review, September 2007
(https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem).*

## Why it works

Klein cites a 1989 study by Deborah Mitchell, Jay Russo and Nancy Pennington: people who imagine an event has
already happened find reasons for it about 30% better than people asked what might happen. "Why did it fail?" gets
better answers than "what could go wrong?".

## It already failed

Start after the plan is clear. State as fact that the project has failed, badly. Everyone now explains a failure
rather than doubting a plan, so criticism stops sounding disloyal.

## Everyone writes alone

For a few minutes each person writes every reason they can think of, alone, especially the ones they would normally
keep quiet about. Then go round the room: each person reads one reason at a time, starting with whoever leads the
project, until every reason is on the list. Writing first keeps the loudest voice from setting the list.

In Klein's examples, a quiet engineer revealed that the software would not run fast enough on the laptops in the
field, and an executive noticed nobody had planned around the company's own decision calendar. Both changed the plan.

## Evidence, not worry

A reason becomes a risk when something points to it: a number in your own data, a customer's words, a company that
failed the same way. Look for it, link it, and say when you found nothing.

## From stories to a register

Merge the duplicates. Score each by how likely and how damaging. Keep the top five; a register of thirty is a
register nobody reads. For each, change the plan so it is less likely or hurts less.

## Early warnings

Klein notes that the exercise also trains the team to notice early signs of trouble. Give each risk a sign you can
see early, a number or a date, and someone who watches it. Revisit the register on set dates.

## When to run it

Before any bet that is hard to undo: a launch, a price change, a big hire, a fundraise, a pivot, a large contract.
Klein describes running it after the team has been briefed on the plan, at the start rather than the end.

## Running it with a small team

Klein's method assumes a room of people. A founder alone can still write their reasons first, then compare with the
list the team in this app writes, and then ask a cofounder, an adviser or an early employee to write theirs alone,
before seeing anyone else's. (This adaptation is this app's, not Klein's.) The rule that matters is the same: each
person writes before anyone reads.
