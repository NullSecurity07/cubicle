# Measuring product-market fit

*A way to put a number on product-market fit and to steer it, written for this app in our own words from First
Round Review's account of how Superhuman built its product-market fit engine
(https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/), which builds on Sean
Ellis's survey question.*

## The four questions

1. How would you feel if you could no longer use the product? Very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, not
   disappointed.
2. What type of people do you think would most benefit from it?
3. What is the main benefit you get from it?
4. How could we improve it for you?

The first gives the score. The other three tell you who loves it, why, and what to fix.

## Who to ask

Only people who have really used it: Superhuman asked those who had used it at least twice in the past two weeks.
Never ask the same person twice, so the number stays comparable. Ask new users continuously and look at the score
weekly, monthly and quarterly.

## The 40% line

Sean Ellis compared nearly a hundred startups and found that those where more than 40% of users would be very
disappointed without the product tended to have strong traction, and those under it tended to struggle. It is a
benchmark, not a law. Superhuman began at 22%.

## Segment before you judge

The overall number hides who loves you. Group the answers by the kind of person, and look at each group's own
score. Superhuman's jumped from 22% to 33% just by looking only at the groups who loved it most (founders, managers,
executives, business development). That is not cheating; it tells you whom to build for.

## The customer who expects the most

Describe the person in the highest-scoring group as a single character: what their day looks like, what they cannot
stand, the one benefit they name. Superhuman's was a busy professional drowning in email who cared most about speed.
Build for that person first.

## Half and half

Ignore the not disappointed: they are not your market yet. Look at the somewhat disappointed, and keep only those whose
main benefit matches your lovers' main benefit. Their asks are what stands between them and loving you. Spend half the
roadmap making the loved thing better, and half removing those blockers. Superhuman's lovers wanted more speed; the
people close to loving it wanted a mobile app, integrations and better search, among others. After three quarters
the score was 58%.

## Reading it with retention

A survey is what people say; retention is what they do. Read the score beside your retention curve (see the Weekly
metrics framework). 2025 data from ChartMogul, reported by Growth Unhinged
(https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-ai-churn-wave), put median gross revenue retention for AI-native products at
40% against a B2B SaaS median net retention of 82%: products people rave about can still be left. If the score is high
and users leave, trust the leaving.
