# Raising a seed round

*How to plan and run a first round. Written for this app in our own words from Geoff Ralston's "A guide to seed
fundraising" (https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising), YC's safe documents
(https://www.ycombinator.com/documents), Janelle Tam's YC guide to Series A pitches
(https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8d-how-to-build-a-great-series-a-pitch-and-deck), and CRV's 2026 data room guide
(https://www.crv.com/content/data-room-setup). Dollar figures in Ralston's guide are years old; use them as shapes,
not prices.*

## When to raise

Raise when you can tell the story: you know who the customer is, you have something they use, and it is growing fast.
Ralston calls 10% a week for several weeks impressive. If you do not have that yet, work on the product and talk to
users.

## How much

Enough to reach profitability, or the next milestone that makes the company fundable again, usually 12 to 18 months
out. Build it bottom-up: monthly cost times months. Ralston's example: 18 months, five engineers, about $15k a month
each, comes to about $1.35M. Give a range and a plan for each end.

## Safe, cap and dilution

Most seed rounds use a safe or a convertible note. A safe works like a note without interest, maturity or repayment;
usually only the amount, the valuation cap and perhaps a discount are negotiated. YC's standard since 2018 is the
post-money safe, with versions for a cap only, a discount only, or "most favoured nation", and an optional pro rata
side letter. Dilution is what you sell over the value after the money: $1M on $5M before is 1 ÷ 6, 16.7%, not 20%.
Ralston's guide: 10% is great, most seed rounds go up to 20%, avoid more than 25%. Let investors set the price; do not
over-optimise it.

## Who to meet

Investors who invest at seed, in your space, without a competitor in their portfolio. Know what each one likes and
why before the meeting.

## Warm first

An introduction from someone they trust beats a cold email. Book many first meetings close together; focus on those
most likely to close.

## Every meeting ends with a next step

The aim of a first meeting is the second. Keep the pitch simple (why this product, why you, why it can be huge), get
them talking, and never leave without asking for the money or agreeing a dated next step. Do not negotiate on the
spot: take requests away.

## Close fast

Once someone says yes, close with standard documents that week. The first cheque is the hardest; each next one is
easier.

## The one-pager

One page: vision, product, team, traction, market size, and minimal financials (revenue, money raised, this raise).
Keep your market figure bottom-up: customers times what each is worth to you.

## The data room

Build it before you start, so every number agrees. Folders: summary, financials, legal (incorporation, cap table,
board papers, contracts), go-to-market, product (roadmap, architecture, IP assignments), people (org chart, option
plan). CRV's advice: share in stages, deck first, more only with real interest, everything near a term sheet; keep it
lean. At seed, an investor who asks for heavy diligence is one Ralston says to be wary of.

## Questions they will ask

Write your story as 10 to 15 claims, each with the numbers that prove it, and list the questions an investor would
ask, above all about metrics. Show trends over months, never cumulative totals.
