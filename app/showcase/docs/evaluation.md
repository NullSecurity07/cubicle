# Evaluation

One idea at a time. Run the five steps in order — assumption, flaws, pain, fit, verdict — and run them again whenever
the idea changes materially, because the core assumption moves when the customer moves.

## The idea, in one sentence

Kindling is a print-and-play kit for indie board-game designers: card, board and token templates that print true on a home printer, a rules‑sheet layout, and a playtest feedback form that sorts what testers say, offered for €39 a month.

## Who exactly (the well, not the puddle)

Solo and two‑person indie board‑game designers preparing a prototype for playtests, conventions or a Kickstarter.

## 1. Core assumption (falsifiable)

Solo or two‑person indie board‑game designers preparing a prototype will pay €39 per month for a print‑and‑play kit that supplies ready‑to‑print components, a rules‑sheet layout, and a play‑test feedback form.

Cheapest way to test it:
1. Build a one‑page landing site with a €39/mo sign‑up button (or free trial).
2. Run a €30, 2‑day targeted ad to board‑game‑design audiences.
3. Track sign‑up conversion; ≥2 % conversion → assumption holds, <1 % → falsified.

## 2. Three specific ways it dies

1. **Print‑quality mismatch** – Home‑printer inkjet/laser limits cause the supplied templates to print incorrectly (wrong dimensions, smudging), making designers abandon the kit.
   - Survivable? **Yes** – can be fixed by redesigning templates or offering a “print‑ready” PDF service.
   - Cheapest test: Run a **5‑designer pilot**: give them the PDF, ask them to print on their own printers, and collect a quick “Did the pieces come out to scale?” survey (≤ €30 for sample prints).

2. **Playtest feedback overload** – The built‑in feedback form generates too much unstructured data; users find it harder to synthesize than manual notes, so they stop using the kit.
   - Survivable? **No** – if the core value (structured feedback) is broken, the kit loses its unique advantage and cannot be easily replaced.
   - Cheapest test: Build a **minimal prototype** of the feedback sorting algorithm (e.g., a Google Sheet with simple categories) and have 3‑5 designers run a playtest, then ask “Did the form help you extract actionable insights?” (no cost beyond time).

3. **Pricing mis‑fit** – €39 /month is too high for hobbyist designers who prefer one‑off purchases; churn spikes after the first month, leaving cash‑flow insufficient.
   - Survivable? **Yes** – can pivot to a per‑project price or a freemium tier.
   - Cheapest test: Offer **2‑week free trials** to 10 designers and track conversion after trial; compare against a **pay‑per‑print** pilot (e.g., €5 per kit). No development needed, just Stripe/PayPal test.

## 3. Is the pain real?

Vitamin — painkiller — hair on fire: **Painkiller**. There is real, recurring friction (fighting print margins, scattered playtest notes) and 12 beta users actively use the kit, with 2 paying €39/month. But the behaviour is interest-plus-some-payment, not "hair on fire": nobody pays anything-today or hacks together an emergency fix. With real payment it clears painkiller, but the evidence is thin.

Well or puddle, and the path out of the well: **Well** — a narrow group (solo and two-person indie designers prepping a prototype for playtests, conventions or Kickstarter) with a concrete, specific need. The path out: win this niche of pre-Kickstarter designers, then grow toward publishers and hobbyists later.

The workaround they use today: Spreadsheets, scattered notes, and DIY home-printing fights — an ugly-workaround signal that the pain exists.

What it costs them per month: Not directly measured. The cost is in lost evenings and prep time per prototype; €39/month is competing against that time cost. Worth quantifying in the next customer conversations.

Real customer conversations done (of ten to twenty): **12 beta users, 40 playtest sessions.** These count as evidence of use, but they are not Mom-Test interviews. The structured conversations ("walk me through the last time", "what do you do today", "how much does it cost you") still need to run to reach 10–20 — and to answer why only 2 of 12 pay.

Payer = user? If not, who pays: Payer = user (the solo designer).

Open pain questions: Why do only 2 of 12 beta users pay? Do designers come back after their prototype is printed (retention), or is the pain per-project rather than monthly?

## 4. Founder-market fit

Lived it / unusual access / technical edge / contrarian insight / earned secret / none: Lived it (self-published two small card games as a solo designer-developer, experiencing the exact pain of fighting print margins and collecting scattered playtest notes).

In one sentence: As a solo indie designer who has self-published two card games, the founder has lived the problem firsthand and possesses direct lived experience of the daily friction of prep and playtesting.

## 5. Verdict

**PIVOT REQUIRED**

Because:
1. **Assumption plausible?** Yes — solo and two-person indie board-game designers preparing a prototype will pay €39 per month for a print-and-play kit (1).
2. **Painkiller or better?** Yes — painkiller; there is real recurring friction (fighting print margins, scattered notes) and 2 paying customers (€39/mo each), though the evidence is thin (3).
3. **Flaws survivable or cheaply tested?** No — one critical flaw (“Playtest feedback overload”) is marked as **Survivable? No** (2), which triggers a cap of **PIVOT REQUIRED** and **6/10**.
4. **Real founder fit?** Yes — lived it (the founder has self-published two small card games as a solo designer-developer and experienced the exact pain firsthand).
