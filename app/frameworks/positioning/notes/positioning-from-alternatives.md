# Positioning from the alternatives up

*How to decide what your product is, for whom and against what. Written for this app in our own words from April
Dunford's positioning method: her quickstart guide
(https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning), a summary of the ten steps in Obviously
Awesome (https://www.heinzmarketing.com/blog/10-step-positioning-process-an-obviously-awesome-book-summary-part-3/),
and her note on the 2026 edition (https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/announcement-obviously-awesome-the).*

## The five parts

Positioning has five parts, and the order matters: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, best-fit
customers, market category. A trend can be added on top. Most teams start with the category; Dunford starts with the
alternatives, because that is where the customer starts.

## Start from happy customers

Look at the customers who love the product and ask why they chose it. They show you what the product is really good
at, which is often not what you built it for.

## What they would do instead

List what customers would do if your product did not exist: a spreadsheet, hiring someone, an agency, another tool,
or nothing. These are your real competitors, and often they are not the companies you worry about.

## Unique attributes, with proof

What do you have that those alternatives do not? A feature, a way of working, a business model, expertise. Each needs
proof a buyer would accept. If the alternatives have it too, it is not unique.

## Value, in the customer's words

For each attribute ask: so what, for the customer? Group the answers into a few value themes. Dunford's 2026 edition
expands this step because it is where teams most often get stuck.

## Who cares most

Which customers care a lot about that value? Describe them by what makes them care (their situation, their problem),
not only by size or industry. Name who is a poor fit too.

## A frame that makes the value obvious

Pick the market category that makes your value obvious to those customers. A category brings expectations with it:
the competitors buyers compare you to, the features they assume, the price they expect. Choose the one where your
strengths look like strengths.

## A trend, carefully

A trend can make the product feel timely, but only if it makes the value clearer. A trend that does not fit
confuses.

## Writing it down

Put the five parts on one page everyone uses: sales, marketing, product. The one-liner, the homepage and the pitch
all come from it. The new edition puts more weight on turning positioning into sales and messaging.
