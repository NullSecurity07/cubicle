# Talking to customers without leading them

*How to learn from a conversation when the other person wants to be nice to you. Written for this app in our own
words from Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test (https://www.momtestbook.com/, summarised at
https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-the-mom-test/) and Eric Migicovsky's YC Startup School talk "How to talk to
users" (https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/).*

## The three rules

People lie to founders, kindly. Ask whether they like your idea and they will say yes, because saying no is rude.
The Mom Test's answer is to ask questions even your mother could not lie to you about:

1. Talk about their life, not your idea. "How do you handle this today?" not "Would you use this?"
2. Ask about specific things that happened, not what they might do. "When did this last happen?" not "Would you pay
   for this?"
3. Talk less. The more you speak, the more you steer them.

## Three big questions

Before a week of conversations, write down the three things you most need to learn. A good big question can prove
your idea wrong: "Do finance leads at 50-person companies lose a day a month to this?" A bad one can only flatter
you: "Is this a good idea?" Everything you ask in the room serves one of the three.

## The five questions

Migicovsky's five, adapted in the plan to your problem:

1. What is the hardest part about doing the thing you are working on?
2. Tell me about the last time you ran into it.
3. Why was that hard?
4. What, if anything, have you done to solve it?
5. What do you not love about the things you tried?

Useful follow-ups from the same talk: how often it happens, what it costs, and whether there is a budget for it.

## Who to talk to first

One narrow group, not everyone. A segment is narrow enough when you could list where to find them tomorrow. Start
with people you can reach warmly, then in person, then cold. Keep it short and casual, ten to fifteen minutes; a
coffee is better than a formal meeting.

## Bad data and how to catch it

- **Compliments** ("love it", "sounds great"). Worth nothing. Steer back: "When did you last deal with this?"
- **Hypotheticals** ("I would", "I might", "I always", "I never"). A prediction, not a fact. Ask for the last time.
- **Feature wishes**. Note them, do not promise them; ask what they were trying to do when they wished for it.

## Signals that count

- A fact with a date: something that happened.
- Pain with a cost: hours, money, a missed deadline, in their words.
- A workaround: a spreadsheet, an intern, a tool they pay for. People who are already spending to fix it are buyers.
- A commitment: a second meeting, an introduction, a trial, a deposit. A conversation that ends with a next step
  advanced; one that ends warm and vague did not.
- Zombies: people who stay friendly and never take a step. Count them apart.

## Reading the pattern

One person is an anecdote. A pattern is three people or more saying the same thing unprompted, about things that
happened. Write findings against the three big questions, not against your idea. If after five conversations nobody
has a workaround or a cost, the problem may be real but not painful, and that is the answer.
