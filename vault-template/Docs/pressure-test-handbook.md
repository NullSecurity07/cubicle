# Pressure-test handbook

How this company judges a startup idea: the Paul Graham / YC test, in the order it has to be run. The board
runs it on every idea card; the founder brief takes it once. `Docs/ideas-handbook.md` is how an idea is scored
and decided — this is how it is *judged*.

The stance: skeptical but not cynical. Assume the idea is wrong and try to prove it. Whatever survives is worth
building. The point is not to predict success, it is to find the fatal flaw early, while fixing it costs a week
instead of a year. Startups do not die from competition; they die because nobody wanted the thing, or because
they ran out of money before finding what people did want.

Run it in this order and in writing: assumption, flaws, pain, fit, verdict. Founders naturally start at "why
I am great" and skip "is the pain real". Re-run it whenever the idea changes materially — the core assumption
moves when the customer moves.

## The core assumption

The one belief that, if false, makes everything else irrelevant. Also called the leap-of-faith assumption, the
riskiest assumption, the load-bearing wall.

- Write it as a **falsifiable sentence**, not a hope. "People want better budgeting" is not one. "Freelancers
  earning 3–10k a month will pay 15 a month to auto-separate tax money" is.
- How to find it: list every assumption the idea rests on (there will be five to fifteen), and for each ask
  "if this turned out to be false, would I still build this?". The first hard **no** is the core assumption.
  The alternate test: what is the thing you are most afraid to go and check?
- It is almost always about **demand**, not technology. "Can we build it?" is rarely the risk.
- One assumption, not three. Three means the root has not been found yet.

Three kinds of idea, worst last. **Organic** — you noticed a problem in your own life or work; most big
companies started here. **Made-up** — you sat down and tried to think of a startup; plausible-sounding and
usually fake. **Trend-chasing** — "X but with AI", "Uber for Y"; a crowded axis with no insight behind it.

A good idea is something the founders themselves want, that they can build, and that few others realise is
worth doing. The third is the hard one, and it is why a good idea often *looks* bad at first.

## The failure catalogue

The three most likely ways this idea dies. **Specific, never generic.** Generic applies to any startup and is
useless: "competition is tough", "user acquisition is hard", "might not scale". Specific names a mechanism
unique to this idea, this market and this customer: "the incumbent already has this buried in settings and will
surface it the month you get traction"; "your buyer is a school principal who buys once a year in March, and
missing it costs twelve months"; "every new city needs 300 supply-side signups before demand works, and you
have no channel to seed supply".

Go through all six buckets and ask, of each, whether it applies to *this* idea.

- **A. Demand.** Nobody has the problem. The problem is real but not painful enough to change behaviour. It is
  real but people will tolerate it rather than pay. It is real for too few people.
- **B. Customer structure.** Payer is not the user (the teacher feels the pain, the district pays). A two-sided
  market with no way to seed the first side. The customer is too poor. The customer cannot be reached — no
  channel, no list, no community.
- **C. Distribution.** No natural channel, so paid ads from day one. A sales cycle longer than the runway. A
  reliance on virality in a product nobody would share. A platform that can cut you off.
- **D. Competition and timing.** An incumbent can add it as a feature in one quarter. It has been tried five
  times and you do not know why they died. Too early: the enabling technology or behaviour is not here yet.
  Too late: the market already consolidated.
- **E. Economics.** Unit economics negative and not improving with scale. Acquisition cost above lifetime value
  with no path to fix it. Structurally low gross margin — services in disguise, hardware, logistics.
- **F. Execution and founders.** It needs a skill nobody here has. It needs capital before revenue. The
  founders do not actually want to do the unglamorous work the idea requires.

Always ask **"why has nobody done this already?"**. Three answers, and they sort ideas fast. *They have, and
died* — find out why, and whether it is still true today. *Nobody thought of it* — suspicious; usually it means
no demand. *Something just changed* — technology, law, behaviour. That last one is the best answer, because it
is the only one that answers "why now?".

Write the three flaws **without rebutting them**; rebutting is a defence mechanism. The flaw that cannot be
answered in one clear sentence is the real one. And look up the graveyard: search the idea plus "shut down" and
"post-mortem", and read at least two.

## Is the pain real?

Place the idea on the line: **vitamin** ("nice to have", interest but no payment and no retention) → **painkiller**
("solves real pain", some pay, retention holds) → **hair on fire** (urgent, they are already hacking a solution
together today). A vitamin is not a startup.

Prefer a **well** to a **puddle**: a few people with a desperate need beat many people with a shallow one. Dig
the well first, then expand outward — but there must be a plausible path outward, or the well is the whole
market.

The five tests:

1. **Existing-solution test.** Is anyone solving this badly right now — a spreadsheet, an intern, a duct-taped
   tool, a paid consultant? An ugly workaround is a strong signal. No workaround at all usually means no pain:
   people find a way when it hurts.
2. **Money test.** Are they already spending money or time on this, and how much?
3. **Disappearance test.** If it vanished tomorrow, would they be angry or shrug?
4. **Frequency test.** Daily or weekly pain beats annual pain, which is forgotten between uses.
5. **Urgency test.** Are they looking for a solution now, or would they get to it eventually?

How to validate, without lying to yourself — the Mom Test. Never ask "would you use this?"; people lie to be
polite. Ask about their life and their past behaviour instead: walk me through the last time this happened;
what do you do about it today; what have you already tried; what does it cost you a month; who else has this
problem. **Compliments are noise. Commitments are signal**, and they run weak to strong: "cool idea" →
"I'd use that" → an email address → an introduction to their boss → an hour on a call → an agreed pilot → money.
Only the last one really counts.

Ten to twenty conversations before writing code, and not with friends. If ten people with the problem cannot be
found, that is itself the result. "Everyone" is not a customer: if the answer to "who is this for" is everyone,
it is a puddle.

## Founder-market fit

The idea will change; the founder will not. This is the step founders skip because it feels self-indulgent.

Acceptable answers to "why you?": you **lived the problem** and felt it daily; you have **unusual access** —
you know 200 of these customers personally, or you are in the community; you have an **unfair technical edge**
and can build what others cannot in a reasonable time; you hold a **contrarian insight** the market believes is
wrong; you have an **earned secret** from years in the industry.

Unacceptable: "I'm passionate about it" (passion is not an edge). "I noticed the problem" (noticing is cheap).
"I've read a lot about the space" (so has the competitor). "I'm a great generalist".

On how many of them: a solo founder is a yellow flag, not a red one; two founders
who have worked together before is the strongest signal there is.

The fit that gets funded is knowing the customer deeply **and** being able to build it. Knowing the customer
without being able to build is fixable with a cofounder. Being able to build without knowing the customer is a
technology looking for a problem. Neither is a tourist: a tourist knows neither the customer nor how to build
for them — no lived problem, no unusual access, no technical edge, no earned secret — and scores 0 on founder fit.

Related: **schlep blindness**. Founders unconsciously avoid ideas that involve tedious, painful work — sales,
regulation, logistics, banks — so the best ideas often sit in plain sight, unclaimed because they look
unpleasant. Ask: is the reason nobody does this that it is hard and boring? If so it is an opportunity, as long
as someone here is willing to do the schlep.

## The three verdicts

Three allowed outcomes, and no fourth. Give **the verdict first — STRONG, WEAK or PIVOT REQUIRED — then the
reasons**. `PIVOT REQUIRED` is two words and is still one verdict; what is banned is hedging, not a space.

- **STRONG** — the core assumption is plausible, the flaws are survivable, the pain is real, the founder fits.
  Build a cheap test now.
- **WEAK** — at least one step fails hard, usually pain or distribution. Do not build it as it stands.
- **PIVOT REQUIRED** — the pain is real but it is being attacked wrongly: wrong customer, wrong wedge, wrong
  angle. Name the wedge to attack instead.

**Banned phrase: "it has potential, but…".** It is a way of avoiding the decision, and it is refused here.

The meta-question, and the only test that matters: **would Paul Graham fund this in its current form** — not
the version it would be after six more months of work.

## The rubric

Quick triage, five dimensions, 0–2 each, so a total out of ten. The board writes it on the card as
`7/10 — core assumption 2, fatal flaws 1, pain 2, founder fit 1, why now 1`. It is advisory: the company's own
keep / park / scrap score in `Docs/ideas-handbook.md` is what decides, and this never moves it.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| core assumption | vague or unfalsifiable | stated, untested | stated, with early evidence |
| fatal flaws | cannot answer any | can answer one or two | all three have a cheap test |
| pain | vitamin | painkiller | hair on fire |
| founder fit | tourist | some access or skill | lived it and can build it |
| why now | no answer | plausible | a clear shift in technology, law or behaviour |

8–10 reads strong, 5–7 reads pivot or needs work, 0–4 reads weak.

## Tarpit ideas

Ideas that look great, attract many founders, and reliably fail: discovery apps ("find things to do this
weekend"), a social network for some niche, recipe and meal-planning apps, generic productivity and
note-taking, event planning, "Uber for X" where X has no urgency. The trap is that the *problem* is real while
the *pain* is shallow — everyone recognises it and nobody pays. The test: search the idea; twenty dead versions
means a tarpit.

## The wedge

Do not launch the full vision. Launch the **narrowest useful slice** to the **most desperate users**, and
expand from there. A `PIVOT REQUIRED` verdict owes a wedge: name the slice, and name who to aim it at.

Early on, do things that do not scale — recruit users one at a time, by hand. Manual means fast learning;
automation is premature. A founder unwilling to do the unscalable thing probably does not want it badly enough.

And note what actually stops a copycat: network effects, switching costs, proprietary data or distribution,
brand (rare this early), and **speed of learning**, which is the only moat a week-one company really has.

## How the board runs it

The board runs this as a dialogue you can read: six rounds, written to
`Meetings/<stamp>-board-<subject>.md` and linked from the note you rule on. In each of the first five rounds —
one per part above — the chair states that round's question in its own words, the other two advisors answer it
in turn (120 words each, having read everything said so far), and the chair rules: one `FINDING:` line, which
is what you read on the card as that part. When the two disagree, or one of them hedges, the chair challenges
them by name once before ruling. Round six is the verdict and the rubric. At most 17 model calls;
`board_dialogue: brief` in the constitution buys the older four-call session instead, with nothing to read but
the answer.

**The question rule: the board may not guess.** When nothing in front of it answers a round, the chair writes
`QUESTION FOR THE OWNER: …` in place of that finding and moves on, and one cabin message — "The board needs N
answers from you" — collects every one of them. The verdict still stands as given. What your answers change is
the next session on that subject: it re-argues only the rounds that asked you something and keeps the rest
verbatim.

## Where this shows up

- Every `Ideas/<slug>.md` card: the board's verdict, its rubric and its findings under `## The board`.
- `Memory/founder-brief.md`: one `## Pressure test` section, the five parts, on the company as a whole.
- The owner's `idea_verdict` note in `00 Inbox/`, which is where the ruling is actually made.
- `Meetings/<stamp>-board-<subject>.md`: the transcript, and a cabin message for anything it had to ask you.
